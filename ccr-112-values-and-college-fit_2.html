Why values, not preferences 6 min

Read this first. It is short, and it changes what the rest of today means.

Everybody picks a school for a reason. Most reasons are borrowed.

Ask a senior why a school is on their list and you will hear one of four answers: it is close, it is cheap, somebody I know goes there, or people have heard of it. Those are real considerations. None of them is a value.

A preference is what you would like. A value is what you would give something up for. That difference is the whole lesson. Preferences change with the weather. Values are still true when the choice gets expensive.

You are about to build a list of schools. Built on preferences, it reshuffles every time you learn something new. Built on three values you actually named, it becomes a test you can run — on a school, a program, a job, and on the people you spend Friday night with.

The move today. Forty values → keep ten → cut to three. The cutting is the point. Anyone can say ten things matter. Naming three means saying out loud what you will trade away.

Cut to three 12 min

Ten at a time is impossible to rank. Two at a time takes a second. The page shows you your own values in pairs — pick the one you would keep if you could only keep one. About twenty-five quick choices.

    Make it real 10 min

    A value you cannot describe in a real situation is still just a word. For each of your three, finish two sentences — one about where you go next, one about the people around you.

    Worked example — Independence. Where I go next this looks like: a place I can get to without asking anyone for a ride, and a program I can change my mind inside of without starting over. With friends this looks like: people who do not take it personally when I need a weekend to myself.

    Why the friendship half is here. The same test works on people. If your top value is Loyalty and your friend group treats plans as optional, that is not a personality clash — it is a values mismatch, and naming it is the first honest thing you can do about it.
    Keep these. These six sentences are what you will score every option against in the next two sections, and they become the fit columns on your options spreadsheet.

    Four doors 12 min

    Four ways out of this building, all of them real. Read all four — including the ones you already decided are not you. Then say, in your own words, what each one would have to look like to be worth your time.

    Nobody in this room is being sorted. This page does not pick a door for you and neither does anyone else. You are being asked to look through all four with your eyes open, because the only bad choice on this page is the one you made without knowing what was behind the other three.
    Every number on this page was checked against an official website in September 2026, and the sources are listed on the spreadsheet. Programs change and some of those official sites are themselves out of date. Before a number goes into a decision, verify it. That is not busywork — that is the skill.

    Build your short list 14 min

    Six options, from at least two different doors. Tap to add the ones worth your time, add your own, then mark which of your three values each one serves. These become the rows of your options spreadsheet.

    Why at least two doors. Not because you are supposed to hedge. Because in April, when one door says no or costs more than the letter implied, a list with only one kind of thing on it is not a list — it is a hope.
